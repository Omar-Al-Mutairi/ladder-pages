Pathfinding: Dijkstra, A* and weighted A*

Three versions of one algorithm. Each keeps a priority queue of frontier cells and always expands the one with the smallest key. The only difference is the key: g alone, g + h, or g + w·h. Watch how much of the grid each one explores.

Name

The terms

Priority key
f(n) = g(n) + w · h(n)

g = cost so far from the start. h = estimated cost to the goal (here Manhattan distance). w = 0 gives Dijkstra, w = 1 gives A*.

Admissible heuristic

h never overestimates the true remaining cost. Manhattan distance is admissible on a 4-connected grid with unit steps, so A* returns a shortest path.

Weighted A*

With w > 1 the search trusts h more, expands fewer cells, and finds a path at most w times the optimal cost (bounded suboptimal).

Cost

Each cell is expanded at most once; with a binary heap the work is O(E log V). A good heuristic does not change the worst case, but it shrinks the explored area a lot in practice.

CS · data structures~25 minsemi-hard

B-tree index: insertions and node splits

Databases store indexes as B-trees because every node is one disk page and the tree stays shallow. Here a node holds at most 3 keys (order 4). When a 4th key arrives, the node splits and its middle key moves up. That is the only way the tree grows taller, so all leaves always stay at the same depth.

The rules

Order m

Each node has at most m children and m − 1 keys. Here m = 4. Every node except the root has at least ⌈m/2⌉ − 1 keys (here 1).

Split

An overflowing node (m keys) splits into two halves; the median key moves up into the parent. If the root splits, a new root appears: the tree grows from the top.

Height
height ≈ ⌈log_m(N)⌉

With 200 keys per page, a billion rows need about 4 page reads.

MIS link

This is how a relational database finds a customer by ID without scanning the table. Index design (which columns get a B-tree) is a core database-administration decision.

ME · mechanisms~25 minsemi-hard

Four-bar linkage: Grashof, motion and coupler curves

Four rigid links joined in a loop. Turn the input crank and every other position follows from one vector equation. Link lengths decide everything: whether the crank can turn fully, how far the output swings, and whether a point on the coupler draws an almost straight line.

Name

The equations

Vector loop
a·e^{iθ₂} + b·e^{iθ₃} = d + c·e^{iθ₄}

Two scalar equations, two unknowns (θ₃, θ₄). Geometrically: B is where the circle of radius b around A meets the circle of radius c around O₄. Two intersections = two assembly branches (open and crossed).

Grashof's law
s + l ≤ p + q

s = shortest, l = longest link. If true, at least one link turns fully. Which one depends on where the shortest link sits: as the crank it gives a crank-rocker, as the ground a drag link, as the coupler a double rocker.

Transmission angle μ

The angle between coupler and rocker. Near 0° or 180° the coupler pushes almost along the rocker and force transmission is poor; designers keep μ roughly between 40° and 140°.

Coupler curves

Points on the coupler trace closed curves of surprising shapes. The Hoekens proportions (2 : 1 : 2.5 : 2.5, point at 5) give a near-straight segment, used in walking machines.

Vibration: damping and resonance

A mass on a spring with a damper, pushed by a force F₀·cos(ωt). Three numbers, m, k and c, decide how it rings, how fast it settles, and how violently it answers when ω gets close to its natural frequency.

Name

The equations

Equation of motion
m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = F₀·cos(ωt)

Solved here numerically with 4th-order Runge–Kutta (step 2 ms).

Natural frequency and damping ratio
ωₙ = √(k/m) · ζ = c / (2√(km))

ζ < 1 underdamped (rings), ζ = 1 critically damped (fastest return, no overshoot), ζ > 1 overdamped.

Magnification factor
X/(F₀/k) = 1 / √((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²), r = ω/ωₙ

Peaks near r = 1. With light damping the peak is about 1/(2ζ).

Phase
φ = atan2(2ζr, 1 − r²)

Below resonance the mass follows the force; at r = 1 it lags 90°; far above it moves opposite to the force.

MIS · decision support~25 minsemi-hard

Inventory policy: EOQ, reorder point and service level

The reorder rules inside every ERP system. Order Q units whenever the inventory position drops to the reorder point s. EOQ balances ordering cost against holding cost; safety stock protects against uncertain demand during the lead time. Run a year of random demand and see whether the textbook promise holds.

Name

The formulas

Economic order quantity
EOQ = √(2·D·S / H)

D = annual demand, S = cost per order, H = holding cost per unit per year. At EOQ, yearly ordering cost equals yearly holding cost.

Reorder point
s = μ·L + z·σ·√L

Expected demand during the lead time plus safety stock. z sets the cycle service level: the chance a replenishment cycle ends without a stockout.

Undershoot
E[undershoot] ≈ (μ² + σ²) / (2μ)

With daily review, inventory drops past s before you notice, so the effective safety stock is smaller. The formula above assumes continuous review.

Fill rate vs cycle service

Fill rate counts units served from stock; cycle service counts cycles without any shortage. A 95% cycle service usually means a fill rate above 99%.

MIS · supply chain information~25 minsemi-hard

The bullwhip effect: why information sharing matters

Four companies in a chain: retailer, wholesaler, distributor, factory. Each one forecasts from the orders it receives and orders to cover its lead time. A small change in customer demand grows into wild swings upstream. Turn on information sharing, where everyone sees the retailer's point-of-sale data, and watch the swings shrink.

Name

Causes and cures

Four causes

Lee, Padmanabhan & Whang (1997): demand-signal processing (forecasting from orders, shown here), order batching, rationing and shortage gaming, and price fluctuations.

One stage, moving-average forecast
Var(q)/Var(D) ≥ 1 + 2L/N + 2L²/N²

L = lead time + one review period, N = weeks in the moving average. Longer lead times or shorter memory amplify more.

Whole chain
no sharing: Π (1 + 2Lᵢ/N + 2Lᵢ²/N²)
sharing: 1 + 2ΣLᵢ/N + 2(ΣLᵢ)²/N²

Without shared data the factors multiply; with shared data the lead times add. Sharing reduces the effect but does not remove it.

MIS cures

Point-of-sale data sharing, EDI, vendor-managed inventory (VMI), collaborative planning (CPFR), and shorter lead times. These are information-system decisions with direct cost effects.
